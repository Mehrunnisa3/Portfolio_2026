## The problem

Finding recreational activities in Surrey means navigating a fragmented
landscape. Rec centre drop-ins, nearby gyms, fitness classes, and local leagues
all live in different places — separate websites, Facebook groups, and word of
mouth. Nothing brings all of this together. And even when residents do find
something, the information available is rarely enough to commit. How many people
are actually going? What skill level are they? Will there be enough players for a
real game? For someone new to Surrey or fitting sport into a busy schedule, that
uncertainty is often enough to stop them going altogether.

Surrey.ca — the city's primary recreation portal — lists drop-in sessions but was
built as a general municipal platform covering everything from property tax to
waste collection and so on. Recreation is one section among many, and the
experience reflects that. Knowing just about the remaining spots tells you nothing
about whether the session will have enough players, what skill level they play at,
or whether showing up alone means you will get to play at all.

This gap is not just a UX problem. For newcomers and immigrants building a life in
a new place, recreational sport is one of the fastest pathways to community and
belonging. When the system fails to surface that opportunity clearly, the people
who need it most are the ones who miss out.

## 01. Research Process

### Problem identification and competitive audit

I began with my own experience — arriving in Surrey as an athlete, unable to find
indoor courts in winter, discovering Surrey.ca through word of mouth two years
after moving, and showing up alone to sessions not knowing if enough players would
be there. This personal experience became the foundation of the research.

A competitive audit of five platforms — Surrey.ca, Meetup, Facebook Groups,
Strava, and Sportiner — confirmed that no existing tool combined session
discovery, attendance visibility, and player connection in one place for Surrey
recreational sports.

### User research

To better understand the experiences of Surrey residents and validate the problem
identified through competitive analysis, a questionnaire was distributed to 7
Surrey residents who play recreational sports. In addition to the primary
research, 17 posts from r/SurreyBC were analysed — documenting the same
frustrations independently raised by strangers over years, confirming the problem
extends beyond the study participants.

## 02. From Wireframes to UI

### User flows

Three user flows were mapped before any screens were designed.

**Flow 1** covers finding and booking a drop-in session — the core journey.
**Flow 2** covers player profiles and open-for-pairing across the attendees
screen. **Flow 3** covers gym and fitness class discovery.

### Design system

A design system was established before touching hi-fi screens. Plus Jakarta Sans
for display headings. Inter for all body text. A 10-colour palette anchored by
Midnight Navy and Electric Blue. An 8-component library including the session card
in three states — good to go, filling up, and session full.

## 03. Usability Testing

### Methodology

A remote unmoderated usability test was conducted with 7 participants using the
System Usability Scale and five post-task questions. Participants were Surrey
residents who play recreational sports. The core task given to all participants was
to find a basketball drop-in session, assess whether it was worth attending, and
complete a booking.

Testing was conducted remotely via questionnaire and voice note explanation.
Results were analysed using thematic analysis across four clusters mirroring the
original research methodology.

### SUS results

Line Up achieved an average SUS score of **87.14 out of 100** — Grade B, Excellent
— significantly above the industry benchmark of 68. Six of seven participants
scored in the excellent to best-imaginable range. One participant scored 62.5,
identified as a session discoverability gap for users less familiar with the app
structure.

### Iterations

Five design changes were made based on testing findings. The most significant were
surfacing waitlist availability directly on the session detail screen, replacing
icon-only sport filters with labelled pills, merging the “who’s going” and
attendees sections, and adding skill level to each player row on the attendees
screen.

## 04. Reflection

Line Up validated that the attendance confidence problem is real and that a
focused, research-backed solution can score significantly above industry usability
benchmarks even at prototype stage.

If I were to continue this project the immediate next step would be implementing
partner request functionality — two participants independently requested this
without prompting, suggesting the open-for-pairing tag meets awareness needs but
leaves users wanting a more direct way to connect before sessions.

A moderated usability test would strengthen the findings. Testing was conducted
remotely without observation, which means friction points not captured through
self-reporting may exist. One participant’s inability to find sessions in the
unmoderated test is the clearest example — a moderated session would have revealed
exactly where the discovery breakdown occurred.

If Line Up were to ship, the metrics I would track are booking completion rate,
return usage rate within 7 days, and whether the open-for-pairing feature
correlates with reduced session abandonment — specifically whether users who see
compatible pairing options are more likely to complete their booking.

This project reinforced that the strongest design decisions are the ones you can
trace directly back to something a real person said. Every feature in Line Up
exists because someone in Surrey — in a survey, in a Reddit post, or in my own
experience — encountered a problem that no existing platform was solving.
